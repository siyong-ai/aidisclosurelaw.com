# Illinois AI Law: Two Statutes, One Missing Rulebook

> **Short answer.** Illinois's AI law for the workplace is **HB 3773** (Public Act 103-0804), amending the Illinois Human Rights Act, in force since **January 1, 2026**. It creates **two separate violations**: using AI that has a discriminatory effect in employment decisions, and **failing to notify** people that AI was used — the second is absolute, no damage or discriminatory effect required. The rules defining what a compliant notice looks like were proposed **May 15, 2026**, withdrawn **June 2, 2026**, and have not been refiled. A second, older statute — **AIVIA** — separately governs AI analysis of recorded video interviews.

If you searched "illinois ai law" looking for a general consumer AI statute, Illinois has not passed one. What it has is the most aggressive *employment* AI notice duty in the country, attached to a civil rights statute with no size threshold.

---

## Who Illinois AI law binds

| You are… | What binds you |
|---|---|
| An employer with **1+ employees in Illinois** for 20+ weeks in the current or preceding calendar year | HB 3773 — both the discrimination violation and the notice violation |
| An employer using AI to analyze recorded video interviews of Illinois applicants | AIVIA (820 ILCS 42) — notice, explanation, consent, and deletion duties |
| A business using AI with **customers** (chatbots, support tooling) | Not this law. Illinois has no general consumer AI disclosure statute; see [Maine](/maine/chatbot-disclosure/) for the strictest chatbot rule |
| A vendor selling HR tools to Illinois employers | Not directly liable under HB 3773, but your customers will demand warrant and indemnity terms — Illinois employers cannot contract away their own statutory exposure |

There is **no revenue threshold, no headcount exemption, and no carve-out for employers headquartered elsewhere**. If you have an Illinois employee, the Act reaches the decision.

---

## The two violations, kept separate

The operative language sits at **775 ILCS 5/2-102(L)**, inside the Human Rights Act's list of civil rights violations:

- **Discriminatory use:** using AI that *has the effect* of discriminating on a protected basis in recruitment, hiring, promotion, renewal, training, discharge, discipline, tenure, or terms and conditions. Intent is irrelevant. Using **ZIP code as a proxy** for a protected class is its own violation — the only question is whether the variable was used.
- **Failure to notify:** not telling people AI was used for those purposes. This one does **not** depend on any discriminatory effect. A perfectly calibrated model still violates the Act if nobody was told it was running.

That second violation is what makes Illinois stricter than every other state: the notice duty is absolute, and it protects **employees and applicants**, not customers.

---

## The rules that never landed

The Illinois Department of Human Rights (IDHR) proposed rules on **May 15, 2026** defining the notice, then **withdrew them on June 2, 2026** — eighteen days later. As of November 2026 they have not been refiled.

The practical consequence: the duty is live, its definition is not, and the withdrawn draft (which contemplated an eight-element notice meeting both the statute and the dead draft's structure) is the best available design target for anyone who wants a notice that holds up either way. The full breakdown of both the statute and the dead draft is on the [HB 3773 page](/illinois/hb-3773/).

## AIVIA: the older Illinois AI law still on the books

The **Artificial Intelligence Video Interview Act** (820 ILCS 42, effective January 1, 2020) was Illinois's first AI statute and remains in force. If you use AI to analyze recorded video interviews of Illinois applicants, you must:

1. **Notify** each applicant before the interview that AI will be used to evaluate it,
2. **explain** how the AI works and what characteristics it uses to evaluate applicants,
3. obtain **consent** to the AI evaluation, and
4. **delete** the interview video within 30 days of an applicant's request.

AIVIA predates generative AI and reads narrow, but it applies to any AI analysis of recorded video — including tools added quietly to existing interview platforms.

## Penalties and enforcement

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Forum | IDHR charge process; administrative or circuit court |
| Theory | A civil rights violation under the IHRA — not a consumer-protection violation |
| Remedies | Actual damages, attorney's fees, injunctive and equitable relief, and the civil penalties the Act authorizes |
| Enforcement climate | No IDHR rules in force, so notice adequacy will be tested case by case |

## What Illinois AI law does *not* do

- **No general chatbot disclosure duty.** → [Maine §1500-DD](/maine/chatbot-disclosure/) has the strictest one, in force since September 2025.
- **No rule for AI-generated humans in advertising.** → [New York GBL §296-b](/new-york/synthetic-performer/) covers ads with synthetic performers.
- **No pre-deployment impact assessments.** Colorado's 2024 framework had them; it was repealed before it took effect. → [Colorado](/colorado/)

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there an Illinois AI law?**
Two, both workplace-focused: HB 3773 (in force January 1, 2026) for AI in employment decisions, and AIVIA (2020) for AI analysis of recorded video interviews. Illinois has no general consumer AI disclosure statute.

**What does Illinois HB 3773 require?**
Employers must notify employees and applicants when AI is used in covered employment decisions, and must not use AI that has a discriminatory effect — including using ZIP code as a proxy for a protected class.

**Does Illinois require an AI disclosure notice?**
Yes, in employment. The failure to notify is itself a civil rights violation, with no need to prove anyone was harmed. The IDHR rules that would have defined the notice format were withdrawn in June 2026.

**Who enforces Illinois's AI law?**
The Illinois Department of Human Rights through its charge process, with remedies up to circuit court.

**Does HB 3773 apply to small employers?**
Yes. The Human Rights Act's definition covers employers with one or more employees in Illinois for 20 or more weeks in the current or preceding calendar year.
