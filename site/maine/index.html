Maine AI Law: No Threshold, No Intent, No Excuses

Maine's AI law is 10 M.R.S. §1500-DD, enacted as LD 1727 (P.L. 2025, ch. 294) and in force since September 24, 2025. It bars using an AI chatbot — or "any other computer technology" — to engage in trade or commerce with a consumer in a way that may mislead a reasonable consumer into believing they are dealing with a human being, unless the consumer is notified clearly and conspicuously that they are not. No employee threshold, no revenue test, no intent element, and no exception for a bot that confesses only when asked. A violation is a violation of the Maine Unfair Trade Practices Act, enforceable by the Attorney General.

If you searched "maine ai law," the odds are good you are actually looking for one of two rules: the chatbot disclosure law above, or the political synthetic-media disclosure that took effect July 29, 2026. They get cited interchangeably and they should not be.

#Who Maine AI law binds

You are…What binds you
Any business — two-person shop or bank — with a chatbot, voice agent, or conversational widget serving Maine consumers§1500-DD, on the same terms regardless of size
A bot with a human name, face, or typing indicator ("Hi, I'm Alex!")The paradigm case: the persona is the disclosure problem
A campaign or committee running political ads with synthetic media of a candidate21-A §1014(5-C) — a different rule, with mandatory wording and a 500% penalty
A broadcaster or newspaper carrying a political adLiable under §1014(5-C) only with actual knowledge the media is synthetic
A business using AI for internal decisions (hiring, pricing)Not this law — see Illinois for the employment notice duty, Colorado for decision tools

The definition reaches wider than "chatbot": any software that simulates human conversation through textual or aural communications — and the prohibition extends to "any other computer technology" beyond chatbots. Voice agents must say it out loud at the start.

#Three details that do the work

"May mislead" is not "does mislead." The standard is the capacity to mislead a reasonable consumer, not proof that one was misled. An unnamed assistant with a human persona meets it.

No threshold of any kind. No employee count, no revenue figure, no chat volume. Maine is the only state so far that skipped the size cutoff entirely.

No intent requirement. You did not have to set out to deceive anyone. The design decision is the violation.

And one citation trap: the section was reallocated from §1500-Y by the Revisor of Statutes. Policies and vendor questionnaires citing §1500-Y are citing a section that no longer exists under that number — the same failure mode as Utah's repealed §13-2-12.

#What "clear and conspicuous" means in practice

The statute defines the failure, not the format: a consumer who could reasonably believe a human is on the other end. Test against these:

  • The notice appears before the first exchange, not at message four.
  • It is visible without scrolling — a footnote below the fold is not conspicuous.
  • It says "not a human", not just "AI-powered," which describes the software rather than answering the question the consumer is actually asking.
  • It survives the handoff — human agent to bot is the most common failure point.
  • It follows the conversation out of the widget: SMS, email, WhatsApp, and voice need their own notice.

Geo-targeting the disclosure to Maine visitors is the wrong answer twice over: visitor state is an unreliable inference, and the cost of disclosing to everyone is a line of text that does not measurably hurt conversion.

#Penalties

Civil penaltySet by the UTPA's general provision; no chatbot-specific fine — Maine attached the duty to an existing statute
Other reliefInjunctive relief, restitution, and the AG's costs
Private right of actionUnder the UTPA, not under §1500-DD itself

Being measured against decades of unfair-practices precedent cuts both ways: it is older and more predictable than a new AI test, and it imports a "reasonable consumer" standard your chatbot persona has to survive.

#The other Maine rule: synthetic media in political ads

21-A M.R.S. §1014(5-C) (LD 517, P.L. 2025 ch. 593, in force July 29, 2026): a political communication that already requires a campaign-finance disclosure, and contains synthetic media of a candidate, must also carry:

THIS COMMUNICATION CONTAINS AUDIO, VIDEO AND/OR IMAGES THAT HAVE BEEN MANIPULATED OR ALTERED

The penalty is the outlier: up to 500% of the expenditure on the offending communication (other disclosure violations in the same section run at 100%). Satire, parody, and technical-clarity edits are excluded. This binds campaigns and committees, not ordinary businesses.

#What Maine AI law does not do

  • No rule for AI-generated humans in advertising. → New York GBL §296-b has that, since June 9, 2026.
  • No employment AI notice duty. → Illinois HB 3773, in force now.
  • No provenance or watermarking duties for AI providers. → California SB 942.

Frequently asked questions

Does Maine have an AI law?

Two, aimed at different people: 10 M.R.S. §1500-DD (chatbot disclosure, in force September 24, 2025, binding any business trading with Maine consumers) and 21-A §1014(5-C) (synthetic media in political ads, in force July 29, 2026, binding campaigns).

What does Maine's chatbot law require?

A clear and conspicuous notice, before the interaction, that the consumer is not engaging with a human — whenever a chatbot or other conversational technology could reasonably pass for one.

Does Maine's AI law apply to small businesses?

Yes. It has no employee count, revenue, or volume threshold — a two-person business with a website widget is covered on the same terms as a bank.

What is the penalty for a chatbot disclosure violation in Maine?

It is a violation of the Maine Unfair Trade Practices Act, enforced by the Attorney General with the UTPA's penalties, injunctions, restitution, and costs.

Why do some sources cite §1500-Y instead of §1500-DD?

The section was reallocated from §1500-Y by the Revisor of Statutes after enactment. §1500-DD is the operative citation; older guidance citing §1500-Y is out of date.
