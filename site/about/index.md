# About This Site: How Every Page Is Verified

> **Short answer.** AI Disclosure Law is an independent, source-first reference on US state AI disclosure and transparency law. We are not a law firm and nothing here is legal advice. What we are is rigorous about the three things most coverage of this area gets wrong: the difference between a signing date and an effective date, the difference between a passed law and a dead one, and the difference between what a statute says and what a compliance vendor is selling.

---

## What this site is

A tracking reference for **state-level AI disclosure obligations** in the United States. Seven states currently have something on the books worth tracking — California, Colorado, Illinois, Maine, New York, Texas, and Utah — and the set changes more often than most people expect: Colorado's first AI act was repealed a month before its own start date, California's two election-AI laws were barred by federal courts, Utah's original disclosure section was repealed and re-codified, and Maine's statute changed section numbers after enactment.

That churn is the reason this site exists. When four authoritative sources give four different effective dates for the same Colorado law, the problem is not that information is unavailable — it is that nobody dates their claims or links their sources.

---

## How each page is built

**Enrolled text first.** Every requirement, threshold, and penalty on this site is taken from the bill as passed or the codified statute — not from a press release, a summary article, or another site's paraphrase. Each page links its primary sources directly: the legislature's bill page, the codified section, the court order where one exists.

**Dates are a chain, not a date.** Signed, effective, phased, delayed, repealed, enjoined. A law's "effective date" can move multiple times before it arrives (Colorado's moved twice, then the law was replaced outright), and a signed law can stop existing as enforceable law (California's AB 2839). Pages report the chain, not just the latest entry.

**A verification date on every page.** Each page carries the date its legal claims were last checked against the official text. When a statute changes, the page changes and so does its date. We do not silently edit.

**Dead laws stay, marked.** When a court enjoins a law or a legislature repeals one, the page stays up and says so — because thousands of articles, vendor policies, and compliance checklists still cite the dead version, and the person searching needs to know they are about to comply with a law that no longer binds anyone.

**One question per page.** Pages are built around what a specific person needs to know — "who has to disclose in Texas," "whether California's AB 2839 still applies," "what Maine's chatbot rule requires" — rather than around a single omnibus overview.

## What this site is not

- **Not a law firm, not lawyers, not legal advice.** Everything here is general information about what statutes say. Compliance decisions need a qualified attorney in the relevant jurisdiction.
- **Not an exhaustive legal database.** We track disclosure and transparency obligations, not every AI-adjacent law in every state. When a state passes something we should be covering, that gap gets filled deliberately rather than by scraping.
- **Not affiliated with any vendor, regulator, or trade group.** No sponsored content, no affiliate placements.

---

## Corrections policy

If a page is wrong — a bad date, a mischaracterized provision, a missed amendment — it gets fixed, with the verification date updated. Corrections and gaps can be reported through the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/siyong-ai/aidisclosurelaw.com), where the page sources and full change history are public: every edit to every page is a visible commit, including who changed what and when.

That last part is deliberate. A site that asks you to trust its dates should be able to show you its history.

---

## How updates happen

Pages are reviewed when any of the following occur: a legislature amends or repeals a tracked statute; a court issues an order affecting enforceability; an agency proposes, adopts, or withdraws implementing rules; or a new effective date arrives. The [In effect page](/in-effect/) is the fastest way to see the current state of play across all seven states.

## Frequently asked questions

**Who publishes AI Disclosure Law?**
An independent publisher tracking US state AI disclosure law. We are not a law firm and are not affiliated with any regulator, vendor, or trade group. The site's sources and change history are public on GitHub.

**Is this legal advice?**
No. Every page is general information about what statutes say, verified against official text. For advice on your own situation, consult a qualified attorney.

**How current is the information?**
Every page states the date it was last verified against the official text. The site is updated when statutes are amended, dates move, rules are proposed or withdrawn, or courts bar enforcement.

**How do I report an error?**
Open an issue or pull request on the public GitHub repository linked above. Corrections update the page and its verification date.

**Which states do you cover?**
Seven with AI disclosure or transparency obligations worth tracking: California, Colorado, Illinois, Maine, New York, Texas, and Utah. Coverage expands as states legislate.
