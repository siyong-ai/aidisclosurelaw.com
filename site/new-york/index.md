# New York AI Law: The First Synthetic Performer Disclosure Rule

> **Short answer.** New York's AI advertising law — **S8420-A / A8887-B**, signed December 11, 2025 as Chapter 617, amending **General Business Law §296-b** — has been in force since **June 9, 2026**. If a visual or audiovisual advertisement distributed to a New York consumer features a **synthetic performer** (a digitally created human who is not any identifiable real person), whoever produced or created the ad must **conspicuously disclose** that fact, when they have **actual knowledge** of it. **$1,000** for a first violation, **$5,000** for each subsequent one, enforced by the Attorney General.

If you searched "new york ai law" expecting a broad AI statute, note what New York actually did: it regulated one specific thing — **fake humans in ads** — and did it first, on a different legal theory from every deepfake law before it. The mere presence of a synthetic performer is the trigger; the ad does not have to be misleading.

---

## Who New York AI law binds

| You are… | What §296-b does to you |
|---|---|
| An **advertiser** whose ads can reach a New York consumer | Duty to disclose synthetic performers in visual/audiovisual ads — and no digital campaign can reliably exclude New York |
| An **agency or production company** that created the ad | Directly liable alongside the advertiser — the duty sits with whoever produced the work |
| A **marketplace or e-commerce brand** with AI-generated models in listings | The real exposure: listing-level, creative-level, volume |
| A **media outlet, platform, or billboard company** | **Exempt** — the distribution layer is deliberately spared |
| Anyone using a **digital replica of a specific real person** | A different law: Civil Rights Law §50-f, the expanded post-mortem publicity right, in force since December 11, 2025 |

The test is the **person, not the picture**. A real actor with an AI-replaced background: no duty. A product-only shot with an AI-generated set: no duty. An AI-generated human holding the product: **disclose**. Audio-only ads and translations of a real actor's voice are excluded, and there is an expressive-work exemption for films and games.

---

## Why e-commerce is the exposure

A national brand shoot with an AI presenter is a one-off decision with legal review attached. A marketplace with 40,000 listings is a different problem, because the practice arrived quietly: AI lifestyle models replacing photography for cost, synthetic presenters in UGC-style video ads bought in volume, avatars narrating product tours, creative regenerated per market. Every one of those is an advertisement distributed to a New York audience by default.

The audit question is not "did we use AI in this campaign" but "**is there a human in this asset, and is that human real**" — asked listing by listing, not campaign by campaign.

**Actual knowledge** is in the statute and is not a loophole: vendor invoices that say "AI-generated model", prompt logs, and asset filenames establish it routinely. Where the standard genuinely helps is the unknowing case — an agency or stock library that delivers synthetic people unlabeled — and the fix there is contractual warranty and indemnity, not a compliance memo.

---

## The companion law: digital replicas of real people

Signed the same day, **S8391 / A8882** expanded the **post-mortem right of publicity** under **Civil Rights Law §50-f**, effective immediately on December 11, 2025: heirs or executors must consent before a deceased person's name, image, likeness, or a vocal or visual **digital replica** is used commercially.

Together the two laws cover both halves of the problem: the **identifiable** person (publicity rights, consent, estates) and the **unidentifiable** one (the synthetic performer disclosure). If your AI-generated person resembles a specific real individual, you have a §50-f problem, not a §296-b disclosure problem.

## Penalties

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Enforcer | New York Attorney General |
| First violation | **$1,000** |
| Each subsequent violation | **$5,000** |
| Private right of action | Not created by this section |
| Media outlets / platforms | Exempt from liability for running the ad |
| Other law unaffected | Civil Rights Law §§50, 50-f, 51 preserved |

The amounts are small relative to a national campaign — which is the wrong way to read them. The Attorney General picks test cases, and the second-order cost (re-shooting a catalogue, pulling live listings, restating agency agreements) is where the money is.

## What New York AI law does *not* do

- **No chatbot disclosure rule.** → [Maine §1500-DD](/maine/chatbot-disclosure/) is the strictest in the country.
- **No notice duty for AI-assisted employment decisions.** → [Illinois HB 3773](/illinois/hb-3773/) has that, in force since January 2026.
- **No platform-level provenance requirements.** → [California SB 942](/california/sb-942/) regulates large generative AI providers; New York regulates the ad, not the model.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a New York AI law?**
For advertising, yes: General Business Law §296-b, in force June 9, 2026, requires conspicuous disclosure when an ad features an AI-generated human. New York has not passed a general AI disclosure statute for other contexts.

**What is a synthetic performer?**
A digital asset created or modified by generative AI or a software algorithm that creates the impression of a performance by a human performer who is not recognizable as any identifiable natural person. A digital replica of a specific real person is a right-of-publicity issue under §50-f instead.

**Do I have to disclose AI-generated models in product listings?**
If the listing is an advertisement reaching New York and features an AI-generated human, yes — and marketplaces are the main exposure precisely because listings are ads distributed at volume.

**Are platforms liable for running a non-compliant ad?**
No. Newspapers, broadcasters, streamers, and billboard companies are exempt; liability concentrates on the advertiser and the agency or production company that created the work.

**What is the penalty?**
$1,000 for a first violation and $5,000 for each subsequent violation, enforced by the New York Attorney General.
