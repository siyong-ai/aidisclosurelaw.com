Colorado AI Law: What Is Actually in Effect in 2026

Colorado's original AI Act (SB 24-205) never took effect. It was repealed and replaced on May 14, 2026 — one month before its own delayed start date — by SB 26-189, the Automated Decision-Making Technology Act. That law keeps the short title "Colorado AI Act" but replaces the entire framework. Its duties start January 1, 2027.

If you searched for the Colorado AI Act's effective date and found four different answers, this is why. The date changed twice, and then the law itself was replaced.

#The three errors almost every Colorado AI law page makes

1. It reports an effective date for a law that was repealed. Dozens of pages still say "the Colorado AI Act takes effect June 30, 2026." That date was real when SB 25B-004 set it in August 2025. It stopped being relevant on May 14, 2026, when SB 26-189 repealed and reenacted the statute before the June 30 date arrived. The obligations in SB 24-205 — the duty of reasonable care, the mandatory impact assessments, the annual reviews — were never operative for a single day.

2. It describes obligations the new law deleted. SB 26-189 removed:

  • the duty of reasonable care to prevent algorithmic discrimination,
  • mandatory pre-deployment impact assessments,
  • annual algorithmic discrimination reviews,
  • risk management programs,
  • the obligation to report discovered discrimination to the Attorney General within 90 days.

If a guide asks you to budget for an impact assessment under Colorado law, it is describing a dead statute. Compliance programs built on the 2024 framework need to be reoriented, not extended.

3. It treats the "Colorado AI Act" as one law. There are two more pieces you need to track: HB 26-1263, the Chatbot Safety Act, which passed the legislature on May 11, 2026 and is moving through the same rulemaking, and the Colorado Privacy Act's own automated decision-making rules, which overlap with SB 26-189 in places.

#Is the Colorado AI Act in effect today?

No. As of November 8, 2026, no Colorado AI disclosure obligation is enforceable. There are 54 days until the duties begin, and the Attorney General has not yet published final rules.

Two things are happening in the meantime that matter to you:

  • Rulemaking. The AG must adopt rules implementing the post-adverse-outcome disclosure requirements and the human review process by January 1, 2027, and has discretionary rulemaking authority over the rest of the statute — including the power to define what "materially influence" means. That definition will decide whether your tool is covered.
  • Chatbot rules. HB 26-1263 adds age estimation, non-human disclosure, teen protections, suicide and self-harm response protocols, and an annual report to the AG. Its obligations are being written in the same rulemaking.

#What to do before January 1, 2027

Work backwards from the disclosure duty, not the risk assessment you may have started in 2025.

  • Inventory your decision tools and classify them. SB 26-189 covers "automated decision-making technology," which is not limited to AI and can include systems older than any LLM. If a tool processes personal data to predict, classify, rank, score, or otherwise assist a decision about a person in a covered domain, it is in scope.
  • Check the exclusions before you assume you are covered. Scheduling, administrative routing, workflow management, tools that merely present information for human review, identity verification and other fraud-prevention uses, and spreadsheets that require human analysis are out.
  • Write the pre-use notice. It must be accessible to people with disabilities and to people with limited English proficiency. This is a design requirement, not a legal footnote.
  • Build the 30-day adverse-outcome process. It has to produce: a description of the decision and the role the technology played, instructions for requesting more information, and an explanation of the right to request personal data and meaningful human review.
  • Stand up a human review workflow that can actually override the system. The statute gives consumers the right to meaningful human review; a queue that rubber-stamps the model output is worse than no process at all.
  • Fix your vendor contracts now. Developers owe you technical documentation, and indemnification clauses that shift liability for Colorado anti-discrimination violations are void as against public policy.
  • Set records retention to three years. Both developers and deployers.

#What about the old SB 24-205 obligations?

They are gone. Specifically:

Duty of reasonable care against algorithmic discriminationRemoved
Pre-deployment impact assessmentRemoved
Annual impact assessment updatesRemoved
Written risk management policy and programRemoved
Report discovered discrimination to AG within 90 daysRemoved
Public statement summarizing high-risk systemsReplaced by developer-to-deployer documentation
Consumer notice before a consequential decisionKept, expanded — now with a 30-day post-adverse-outcome disclosure
Right to appeal and human reviewKept — now framed as data access, correction, and meaningful human review

Liability is now allocated between developers and deployers based on relative fault, and developer liability is generally limited to uses that were intended or marketed.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Colorado AI Act in effect?

No. SB 26-189 was signed May 14, 2026 and takes effect January 1, 2027. The 2024 law it replaced never became operative.

When did the Colorado AI Act take effect?

It did not. The original date was February 1, 2026, moved to June 30, 2026 in August 2025, and then replaced entirely before either date arrived. The operative date for the current law is January 1, 2027.

What replaced the Colorado AI Act?

SB 26-189, the Automated Decision-Making Technology Act. It keeps the "Colorado AI Act" short title, so search results mix the two. If a page mentions impact assessments or a 90-day discrimination report, it is describing the repealed version.

Does SB 26-189 apply to my company?

It depends on whether you develop or deploy technology that processes Colorado residents' personal data to materially influence a consequential decision in education, employment, real estate, financial and lending services, insurance and access to benefits, health care, or essential government services and public benefits. There is no employee-count or revenue threshold. For employers, it reaches current Colorado employees and job applicants.

Is there a private right of action?

No. Enforcement rests exclusively with the Attorney General under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act. A violation is treated as a deceptive trade practice, and the AG must give a 60-day notice and cure period before bringing an enforcement action through January 1, 2030.

What is the penalty?

Civil penalties run under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act. The repealed framework set $20,000 per violation; confirm the applicable amount against the current statute and AG rules before you rely on a number.
