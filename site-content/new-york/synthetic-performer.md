# New York Requires Ads to Disclose AI-Generated Humans

> **Short answer.** **S8420-A / A8887-B**, signed **December 11, 2025** as Chapter 617, in force since **June 9, 2026**, amends **General Business Law §296-b**. If a **visual or audiovisual advertisement** for property or services, in any medium, distributed to a consumer in New York, features a **synthetic performer** — a digitally created human who is not any identifiable real person — the person who produced or created the ad must **conspicuously disclose** that fact, when they have **actual knowledge** of it. Penalties are **$1,000** for a first violation and **$5,000** for each subsequent one, enforced by the Attorney General.

New York is the first state to make the **mere presence** of an AI-generated human in an ad a disclosure trigger, independent of whether the ad is misleading. That is a different regulatory theory from every deepfake law that came before it.

---

## The test is the person, not the picture

Most advertising disclosure rules ask whether the **content** is misleading. This one asks whether a **person in the frame is real**.

| The ad contains | Disclosure required? |
|---|---|
| An AI-generated human holding the product, looking like a model | **Yes** |
| A real actor, background replaced with AI | No — the human is real |
| A product-only shot, AI-generated set | No — no human likeness |
| A real actor, voice translated by AI | No — translation exemption |
| An AI-generated human in a promo for a film or game, used as in the work | No — expressive-work exemption |
| Audio-only ad with an AI-generated voice | No — audio-only ads are excluded |
| A synthetic influencer posting organic-feeling content for a brand | Likely yes, if it is an advertisement reaching New York |

The definition: a **synthetic performer** is a digital asset created, reproduced, or modified by computer using generative AI or a software algorithm, intended to create the impression of an audiovisual and/or visual performance by a human performer **who is not recognizable as any identifiable natural performer**.

The last clause matters. A digital replica of a specific real person is a **right of publicity** problem, not this one. This statute is about the human who is nobody — the AI model, the AI spokesperson, the fabricated presenter.

---

## Why e-commerce is the exposure, not the brand campaign

A national brand shoot with an AI presenter is a one-off decision with a legal review attached. A marketplace with 40,000 listings is a different problem, because the practice arrived quietly:

- AI-generated lifestyle models holding products, replacing photography for cost reasons
- Synthetic presenters in UGC-style video ads, bought in volume from agencies
- AI avatars narrating product tours
- Localised ad creative where the model was regenerated per market

Every one of those is an advertisement featuring a synthetic performer, distributed to a New York audience by default — because no digital campaign can reliably exclude New York, and nobody wants to.

The practical audit is therefore **listing-level and creative-level**, not campaign-level. The question to put to the team is not "did we use AI in this campaign" but "is there a human in this asset, and is that human real".

---

## "Actual knowledge" is in the statute, and it is not a loophole

The duty attaches when the advertiser **has actual knowledge** that the ad contains a synthetic performer.

Read carefully, this is narrower than strict liability and narrower than it first appears — but it is not a defence you want to rely on. Actual knowledge is established by ordinary business records: the vendor invoice that says "AI-generated model", the prompt log, the Creative team's Slack thread, the asset filename. In an organisation that knowingly commissions AI models, knowledge is not seriously contestable.

Where the standard genuinely helps is the **unknowing** case: an agency delivers a shoot and does not disclose that some frames were generated, or a licensed stock library supplies synthetic people without labelling them. The fix is contractual — warranty and indemnity in the production agreement — not a compliance memo.

---

## What "conspicuous" has to mean

The statute requires conspicuous disclosure and does not prescribe wording or placement. Enforcement will be fact-specific, so design to the plainest reading: a viewer who is not looking for a disclaimer should still register it.

| Format | What survives scrutiny | What does not |
|---|---|---|
| Video | On-screen text for the duration of the synthetic performer's appearance, legible at mobile size | A 1-second flash at the end |
| Static image ad | Text on the creative, contrasting, readable at thumbnail size | A caption below the platform's "sponsored" label |
| Social video | Disclosure in the creative itself | Reliance on the platform's own "AI-generated" toggle |
| Carousel / stories | On each frame containing the performer | First frame only |

The platform labelling point is the one teams push back on. Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube all offer AI-content labels, and it is tempting to treat them as compliance. They are not: they are platform policies, applied inconsistently, and visible in different places from the ad. The obligation in §296-b sits with the person who produced the ad.

---

## Who is not liable

The statute deliberately spares the distribution layer. Newspapers, magazines, television networks, streaming services, cable systems, billboard companies, and transit advertisers are **not liable** for publishing or disseminating a non-compliant ad.

That concentrates liability on two parties: the **advertiser**, and the **agency or production company** that created the work. Which is why the second-order effect of this law is contractual — production agreements now need to allocate who warrants that no synthetic performer is present, and who pays if one is.

---

## Penalties

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Enforcer | New York Attorney General |
| First violation | **$1,000** |
| Each subsequent violation | **$5,000** |
| Private right of action | Not created by this section |
| Media outlets / platforms | Exempt from liability for running the ad |
| Other law unaffected | Civil Rights Law §§50, 50-f, 51, and §230 of the Communications Act are expressly preserved |

The penalty amounts are small relative to a national campaign, which is the wrong way to read them. The Attorney General picks test cases, and the second-order cost — re-shooting a catalogue, pulling live listings, restating agency agreements — is where the money is.

---

## The companion law, effective the same day

Governor Hochul signed **S8391 / A8882** at the same time, expanding the **post-mortem right of publicity** under **Civil Rights Law §50-f**. Heirs or executors must consent before a deceased person's name, image, or likeness — including a vocal or visual **digital replica** — is used commercially.

That one took effect **immediately on December 11, 2025**, six months before the advertising disclosure rule. It applies to estates and to anyone producing digital replicas of identifiable people, and it is a separate exposure from §296-b.

Together the two laws cover both halves of the problem: the **identifiable** person (publicity rights, consent, estates) and the **unidentifiable** one (disclosure).

## Implementation checklist

- [ ] Classify every live creative: is there a human in frame, and is that human real
- [ ] For each synthetic performer, add a conspicuous on-creative disclosure for the full duration of their appearance
- [ ] Audit product listings and marketplace imagery, not just campaign assets — set a date to finish
- [ ] Do not rely on platform AI labels as the disclosure
- [ ] Put a warranty into production and agency agreements: no synthetic performer without disclosure, with indemnity
- [ ] Require vendors to label synthetic assets at delivery, in the filename and the metadata
- [ ] Add an AI-performer check to the creative approval gate, before trafficking
- [ ] Train media buyers: any placement that can reach New York is in scope, including programmatic
- [ ] Separately review digital replicas of identifiable people under Civil Rights Law §50-f
- [ ] Diary the first enforcement actions; the Attorney General has not yet issued format guidance

## Frequently asked questions

**Is the New York synthetic performer law in effect?**
Yes, since June 9, 2026. Signed December 11, 2025.

**Does it apply to companies outside New York?**
Yes. It reaches any advertisement distributed to a New York audience, wherever the advertiser is located. For digital campaigns that effectively means everywhere.

**What exactly is a synthetic performer?**
A digital asset created, reproduced, or modified by computer with generative AI or a software algorithm, intended to look like a human performer who is not any identifiable real person. AI models, AI spokespeople, and fabricated influencers qualify.

**Do we need to disclose if we use AI to retouch a real model?**
No. The trigger is a human who is not real. Enhancing an image of a real person, or replacing a background, does not create a synthetic performer.

**What about audio ads?**
Excluded. The statute covers visual and audiovisual advertising, and audio-only ads are exempt.

**Are the platforms liable if our ad is missing the disclosure?**
No. Media outlets and ad platforms that publish or run the ad are exempt. Liability sits with the advertiser and the producer or creator of the ad.

**What is the penalty?**
$1,000 for the first violation, $5,000 for each subsequent violation, enforced by the New York Attorney General.
