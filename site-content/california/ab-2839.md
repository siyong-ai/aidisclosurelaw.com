# Is California's AB 2839 Still Enforceable? No — Here Is What Still Applies

> **Short answer.** **No.** AB 2839 was permanently enjoined on August 29, 2025 in *Kohls v. Bonta* for violating the First Amendment, and California did not appeal that judgment. AB 2655 was declared preempted by Section 230 on August 20, 2025, with a stipulated order barring enforcement against any interactive computer service provider. The election-AI disclosure rule California **can** still enforce is **Elections Code §20010**, which runs through December 31, 2030.

If a guide is telling you how to comply with AB 2839, it is describing a law a federal court has barred the state from enforcing. Here is the actual state of play.

---

## What AB 2839 was, and what happened to it

**What it did.** Signed September 17, 2024, AB 2839 banned the knowing distribution of "materially deceptive" AI-generated or digitally altered election content about candidates, election officials, and voting equipment, in the **120 days before** an election and up to **60 days after** for officials and equipment. It allowed satire and parody only if labeled, and gave candidates and elections officials a private right of action.

**What happened.**

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| October 2, 2024 | Preliminary injunction issued — two weeks after signing |
| August 29, 2025 | Summary judgment for the challengers; permanent injunction. The court held the law discriminates based on content, viewpoint, and speaker, and is not narrowly tailored. |
| — | **California did not appeal this ruling.** |

The court's reasoning is worth knowing because it tells you where the boundary is: the law punished speech only when it could harm a candidate's electoral prospects while leaving favorable deepfake portrayals untouched, and "counter-speech" — the subject publicly calling the content false — is a less restrictive alternative to prohibition.

**Status: unenforceable, and not on appeal.**

---

## What AB 2655 was, and what happened to it

**What it did.** The Defending Democracy from Deepfake Deception Act of 2024, codified at Elections Code §20510 et seq., required large online platforms — 1 million or more California users — to identify, label, or remove materially deceptive AI-generated election content during set windows around elections.

**What happened.**

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| August 20, 2025 | Final judgment: preempted by Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act as applied to X and Rumble |
| August 28, 2025 | Stipulated order bars the state from enforcing AB 2655 **in its entirety** against any provider of an interactive computer service |
| September 19, 2025 | California appeals — Ninth Circuit **No. 25-6138** |
| As of October 29, 2026 | Briefed, **no argument date on the Ninth Circuit calendar** |

**Status: unenforceable against platforms, subject to a pending appeal.** The appeal does not stay the injunction.

---

## What California can still enforce

Three rules survive, and only these three matter in practice.

### 1. Elections Code §20010 — the one that works

Anyone who distributes materially deceptive audio or visual media depicting a candidate within **60 days of an election** must include a clear disclosure that the content has been manipulated. Subdivision (c) lets the depicted candidate seek injunctive or other equitable relief plus general or special damages, with attorney's fees available to the prevailing party.

**Sunset:** AB 686 (Chapter 334, Statutes of 2026, approved September 19, 2026) extended the section to remain in effect until **January 1, 2031** — that is, through December 31, 2030. A much narrower successor version becomes operative the same day, reverting to a pre-AI rule about superimposed candidate images with a different disclaimer.

### 2. AB 2355 — political committee ads

A political committee that creates, publishes, or distributes a campaign ad whose images, audio, or video were generated or substantially altered using AI must include a clear disclosure stating AI was used. It applies **only to committees defined under California law, not to individual citizens or individual creators**. Formatting rules differ for print, audio, and video. Penalties up to **$5,000 per violation**, enforced by the campaign-finance regulator.

This narrow scope is why the 2026 Los Angeles mayoral race saw viral AI videos of a candidate go unlabeled — they were made by individual supporters, not committees.

### 3. Elections Code §20010's own disclosure wording

The required disclosure under §20010 is not the same as AB 2355's. If you are running a committee ad inside the 60-day window with manipulated candidate media, you may owe both.

## The practical question: what do I actually have to do?

| If you are… | Your obligation today |
|---|---|
| A political committee running an ad with AI-generated content | Disclose under AB 2355. Up to $5,000 per violation. |
| Anyone distributing manipulated candidate audio/video within 60 days of an election | Disclose under §20010. Candidate can sue for damages and fees. |
| A platform hosting user content | No enforceable California duty. AB 2655 is barred; the appeal is pending. |
| An individual poster or creator | AB 2355 does not apply to you. §20010 disclosure is the rule that reaches distribution. |
| A campaign relying on AB 2839's prohibition | There is no prohibition to rely on. It is permanently enjoined. |

## Why this matters beyond California

Two lessons transfer to every other state's election-AI law:

**Bans fare badly; disclosure survives.** The laws that have held up are narrow disclosure rules tied to short pre-election windows with modest penalties and explicit satire carve-outs — Arizona's SB 1359 model. The laws that fell were broad prohibitions on "materially deceptive" content, which swept in protected commentary.

**Section 230 is still a wall for platform duties.** AB 2655 was not struck down for being substantively wrong. It was preempted because it made platforms liable for user content. Any state law that puts the duty on the hosting platform faces the same argument.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is AB 2839 currently enforceable?**
No. It was permanently enjoined on August 29, 2025, and California did not appeal that judgment. It remains unenforceable absent a reversal that is not pending.

**Is AB 2655 enforceable?**
Not against interactive computer service providers. A stipulated order bars enforcement in its entirety against them. California's appeal is pending at the Ninth Circuit, No. 25-6138, with no argument date as of late October 2026.

**What California election deepfake law is still in force?**
Elections Code §20010 — the 60-day pre-election disclosure requirement for materially deceptive audio or visual media depicting a candidate — extended through December 31, 2030 by AB 686. AB 2355 also remains in force, but only for political committees.

**Did AB 2839's injunction apply to everyone?**
The permanent injunction was entered against the plaintiffs in *Kohls v. Bonta*. In practice the state has no path to enforcement, which is why the practical answer is "unenforceable."

**Does AB 2355 apply to a viral video made by a supporter?**
No. It applies to political committees as defined under California law. Individual creators are outside it.
