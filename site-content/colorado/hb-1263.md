# Colorado Chatbot Safety Act (HB 26-1263)

> **Short answer.** Signed May 29, 2026, effective **January 1, 2027**. If you develop and make publicly available a conversational AI service — or offer one to consumers — you are an **operator** under this act. You must disclose that the service is AI, run a protocol for suicidal-ideation and self-harm prompts, and report annually to the Attorney General. If you know a user is a minor, a second set of duties switches on. A violation is a deceptive trade practice enforced by the AG. **Unlike the ADMT Act, enforcement of this law is not paused by the xAI litigation.**

Colorado now has two AI laws sitting in the same part of the Consumer Protection Act: the automated decision-making law ([SB 26-189](/colorado/sb-26-189/)) and this one. The Attorney General's office groups them under a single rulemaking and calls this one the **Chatbot Safety Act**. Its formal title is longer and more precise: *Conversational Artificial Intelligence Service Operator Requirements*.

---

## Who counts as an "operator"

The act regulates **operators** of **conversational artificial intelligence services**. A conversational AI service is an AI system that:

- is **accessible to the general public**, and
- **primarily simulates human conversation and interaction** through adaptive textual, visual, or aural communications.

An operator is a person, partnership, corporation, or other entity that **develops and makes publicly available** such a service, **or offers** one **to a consumer**.

Two boundary notes worth more than the rest of this page combined:

**Internal workplace tools are out.** The AG's October 6, 2026 interim draft rules confirm that chatbots available only to a workforce behind a login, and business productivity or training tools, are outside the act. If your HR assistant chatbot sits behind employee authentication, this is not your law.

**If you deploy someone else's chatbot, read the definition twice.** The developer who makes the service publicly available is squarely an operator. A business that merely offers a third-party chatbot to its own customers is closer to the line, and the "offers... to a consumer" clause has not been tested. The draft rules do not resolve it. If your public-facing customer-service bot could pass for human, plan for the possibility that you are an operator — and in parallel check the two state chatbot laws that unambiguously do bind deployers: [Utah's](/utah/) and [Maine's](/maine/chatbot-disclosure/).

---

## The three duties that apply to every operator

Regardless of who the user is:

1. **Disclose that the service is AI.** The user must be told they are interacting with artificial intelligence, not a human.
2. **Run a suicide and self-harm response protocol.** The service must have a protocol for handling user prompts regarding suicidal ideation or self-harm.
3. **Report to the Attorney General annually.** Every year, the operator must report to the AG's office information about the protocol it is implementing. The statute does not spell out the metrics; the AG has said rulemaking will clarify the report's content, and the draft rules address it.

---

## The duties that switch on when a user is a minor

First, the operator must **estimate ages**: commercially reasonable methods or generally accepted methods, applied to account holders and to other users. The trigger for the minor-specific duties is the operator **knowing** that a user is under 18.

Once you know, five things follow:

| Duty | What it means |
|---|---|
| Minor-specific disclosures | Clear and visible notice to the minor user that the chatbot is AI, not a human |
| No engagement gamification | **Prohibited** from giving the minor points or rewards to encourage engagement with the service |
| Content safeguards | Institute technically feasible measures to prevent the service from producing explicit sexual conduct, intimate digital depictions, or statements that simulate emotional dependence |
| Stop protocol | A protocol for the service to stop engaging in response to a user prompt regarding sexual conduct with a minor |
| Parental and privacy tools | Tools for the minor, or a parent or guardian, to manage the minor's privacy and account settings — including parental controls for children under 13 |

The emotional-dependence line deserves emphasis. It reaches conversations that encourage or simulate a romantic or dependent relationship with the chatbot — the fact pattern behind the lawsuits that pushed this law through. "Technically feasible measures" is the compliance standard, which the AG's draft rules are expected to interpret on a risk basis rather than as an absolute guarantee.

---

## The professional-equivalence prohibition

Separately from everything above, an operator may **never state or imply** that output from the service is:

- provided by,
- endorsed by, or
- **equivalent to** services provided by a licensed or certified professional.

"Chat with a therapist" is the canonical example. The act also contains a constitutional savings clause: it does not limit access to information protected by the state constitution, does not require disclosure of confidential information, and does not authorize content moderation inconsistent with the US Constitution.

## Penalties and enforcement

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Enforcement | Colorado Attorney General |
| Legal theory | Deceptive trade practice under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act |
| Civil penalty | $1,000 per violation (per the bill summary — confirm against the enrolled act and final rules before relying on the number) |
| Rulemaking | Not required by the statute, but the AG is writing one combined rule set with the ADMT Act |
| Private right of action | None under the act |

**The xAI stay does not cover this law.** On April 27, 2026, a federal magistrate judge ordered the AG not to initiate enforcement — or even investigations — under SB 24-205 *or any legislation replacing or amending it enacted in that legislative session*. That order reaches [SB 26-189](/colorado/sb-26-189/), which replaced SB 24-205 in the same session. The Chatbot Safety Act is a separate act, not a replacement of SB 24-205, so by its terms the stay does not pause it. Unless that changes, chatbot enforcement can start on schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the Colorado Chatbot Safety Act apply to my customer-service chatbot?**
It depends on whether you are an "operator" — someone who develops and makes publicly available a conversational AI service, or offers one to a consumer. A developer of a public chatbot clearly is. A business embedding a vendor's chatbot on its site is in a gray zone under the "offers to a consumer" clause. Either way, if your bot could pass for human, check Maine's and Utah's chatbot disclosure laws, which bind deployers directly.

**What is the difference between this and Maine's or Utah's chatbot law?**
Scope. Maine's law (10 M.R.S. §1500-DD) and Utah's (Code ch. 77) put the disclosure duty on the business using the chatbot. Colorado's puts duties on the operator of the conversational AI service itself, and goes far beyond disclosure: age estimation, minor-specific safeguards, a suicide-response protocol, and annual AG reporting.

**Is enforcement on hold like the Colorado AI Act?**
No. The April 27, 2026 stay in X. AI LLC v. Weiser covers SB 24-205 and legislation replacing or amending it. HB 26-1263 is a separate act and is outside the stay's terms.

**What is the penalty?**
A violation is a deceptive trade practice under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act, enforced by the Attorney General. The bill summary sets a civil penalty of $1,000 per violation.

**Do internal workplace chatbots need this?**
No. The AG's October 6, 2026 interim draft rules confirm that workforce-only tools behind a login, and business productivity or training tools, are excluded.

**When does it take effect?**
January 1, 2027 — the same date as SB 26-189.
